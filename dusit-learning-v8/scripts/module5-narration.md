## Slide 6 (s-methods-a). Cost-based and demand-based pricing

### Reading text

> The first method is cost-based. You calculate what the room costs to provide, not just housekeeping, but a fair allocation of fixed costs like staff, utilities and maintenance, and add a mark-up. That gives you a minimum rate, a floor you never price below, whatever demand is doing.
>
> The risk is the opposite problem. Cost plus mark-up, with no eye on demand, leaves money on the table every time the market is strong.
>
> The second method is demand-based, and this is the primary approach in revenue management. When demand is strong and rooms are filling fast, you raise the rate. When demand is weak, you lower it just enough to stimulate bookings. This is what the Revenue Manager is doing when they move BAR. They are responding to booking pace, competitor occupancy, and events in the city.
>
> Its risk is the mirror image. Demand-based pricing with no cost floor can slip below break-even in a desperate week.

## Slide 7 (s-methods-b). Value, competition, and reference pricing

### Reading text

> Three more pricing methods, all of them about perception rather than pure economics.
>
> Value-based pricing sets rates on what the guest believes the experience is worth. A room with a direct sea view is worth more than the same room type facing the car park. Not because it costs more to clean. Because the guest perceives more value.
>
> Competition-based pricing looks at what comparable hotels are charging. If your comp set is at two hundred and you are at a hundred and forty, you may simply be underpricing your product. Or you may have a reason. Knowing which is the job.
>
> Reference pricing is about the guest's expectation. They paid a hundred and fifty last time, and they will anchor against that when they see two hundred this year. How you communicate the upgrade in value determines whether they feel it is fair, or feel deceived.
>
> Dusit uses all five in combination. Demand-based for daily BAR decisions, value-based for premium positioning, competition-based for market calibration, cost-based to protect the floor, and reference-based in how every offer is communicated.

## Slide 9 (s-ascending). Prices go up, not down

### Reading text

> The ascending strategy is simple. Set a low entry rate at thirty days out to build base occupancy, then raise the rate in steps as arrival approaches and available rooms decrease. By the last three days the hotel is running at its highest rates. Four reasons this works.
>
> One. Guests who book late are less sensitive to price. They need a room, and the scarcity of what is left justifies the premium.
>
> Two. It prevents cancel-and-rebook. A guest who watches prices rising will not gamble on a cheaper rate appearing later.
>
> Three. Price confidence. Consistent upward movement tells the market that Dusit rooms are in demand and hold their value.
>
> Four. Brand equity. Last-minute discounts train guests to wait for the deal, and that erodes your pricing power permanently.
>
> So what not to do is drop prices in the last seven days to fill rooms. It rarely generates the volume needed to compensate for the rate reduction.
>
> Because you are not just pricing one night. You are managing guest expectations across thousands of future bookings.

## Slide 10 (s-exception). The exception: when repositioning is right

### Reading text

> There is one valid exception. When something significant happens that fundamentally changes the demand picture for a period, a major conference cancels, a large group pulls out, an external event removes a demand driver, then the Revenue Manager may reposition.
>
> Repositioning has three steps, in order.
>
> One. Diagnose the real cause of the demand gap.
>
> Two. Set a new, lower BAR deliberately, with Revenue Manager approval.
>
> Three. Resume the ascending strategy from that new base.
>
> What repositioning is not, is a reflexive rate drop because the hotel feels slow. That is panic pricing, and it almost always costs more than it recovers.
>
> The difference is intention and process. Repositioning is deliberate, approved and monitored. Panic discounting is an emotional reaction to a slow day. One is strategy. The other is guessing.

## Slide 12 (s-principles). Customer-centric and optimisation-focused

### Reading text

> The jam experiment is a well-known study in behavioural economics. A supermarket offered twenty-four varieties of jam. A lot of people stopped to look, and three percent bought something. The same supermarket offered six varieties. Forty percent stopped. Thirty percent bought. Ten times the sales, from a quarter of the choice.
>
> The lesson for hotel pricing sits on the customer-centric side. Pricing is not emotional, it runs on data. Perceived value has to justify the price. And offers must stay simple, because confusion reduces conversion.
>
> On the optimisation side, rates must stay yieldable so the Revenue Manager can move them. The rate has to feel fair to the guest and still deliver profit to the hotel. And you balance simplicity for the guest against flexibility for the RM and consistency across channels.
>
> Which is why Dusit's public rate structure has four rates. Four. Flexible, Advance Saver, Early Bird, Stay Longer. That is enough choice for every type of guest commitment, without overwhelming them into booking somewhere simpler. Customer-centric pricing is not just about being fair. It is about being clear.

## Slide 15 (s-lfk). Leaders, Fillers, and Killers

### Reading text

> Every component in a package is one of three things.
>
> A Leader is what the guest specifically wants and will pay for. A dinner at your signature restaurant. A sixty-minute spa treatment. An airport transfer for a long-haul arrival. Without a leader, the package is just a room with extras the guest did not ask for.
>
> A Filler feels good to receive but would not change the booking decision on its own. Breakfast, a late checkout, a welcome amenity. Fillers add perceived value at low marginal cost. They make the package feel complete without costing the hotel much.
>
> A Killer is the component that makes the guest step back. A mandatory pet fee in a package aimed at business travellers. A compulsory gala dinner they never wanted. A parking surcharge at a city hotel where most guests do not drive.
>
> One killer can undo every leader and filler in the package. So the formula is simple. Leaders plus fillers, no killers.
>
> And a maximum of three add-ons. Any more and the offer becomes complicated, and complicated offers do not sell.

## Slide 18 (s-scenario). Should you drop the rate? (branching GATE)

> A competitor drops their rate. You are below last year's pace. The GM is nervous. The Sales team wants to act. Should you drop the rate?
>
